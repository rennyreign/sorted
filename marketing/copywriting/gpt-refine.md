Yes. I’d give the GPT editor this as an **evolution/addendum to the existing doctrine**, rather than replacing the original Hermetic Revelation framework. The original principles remain right; this captures what we discovered about how they should actually sound in practice. 

You can pass the following directly to the editor:

> ## Doctrine Refinement: Own the Observation
>
> The most important refinement to Hermetic Revelation advertising is this:
>
> **Do not write an observation in order to earn permission to sell the product.**
>
> The observation should exist because it is true, interesting or useful in its own right.
>
> A common failure mode is technically following SALT → OBSERVATION → REVELATION → OFFER while allowing the reader to feel the commercial machinery underneath it. The writer establishes a reasonable premise, explains why it matters, reveals an insight, and then introduces the product that conveniently solves it.
>
> This can still feel like conventional advertising with a more sophisticated setup.
>
> Instead, **own the observation.**
>
> Say the thing because it is worth saying. Follow the thought where it naturally goes. Do not constantly explain why the reader should find it interesting or why it matters.
>
> The offer can then enter somewhere inside that reality because the advertiser genuinely has something relevant to offer.
>
> The desired feeling is not:
>
> **“Here is an insight that proves why you need our product.”**
>
> It is:
>
> **“Here is something true about your world. We understand it. And, separately but relevantly, we have something you may want.”**
>
> The distinction is subtle but fundamental.
>
> ---
>
> ## The Emotional Existence Test
>
> The existing Existence Test asks:
>
> **If there were no product at the end, would this observation still have been worth sharing?**
>
> Strengthen this test.
>
> It is not enough for the answer to be technically yes.
>
> The copy must **feel** as though the writer would still have made the observation if there were nothing to sell.
>
> Readers should not be able to smell the setup.
>
> Avoid writing backwards from the product:
>
> 1. What are we selling?
> 2. What problem does it solve?
> 3. What insight can we construct around that problem?
>
> Prefer:
>
> 1. What is actually true here?
> 2. What have we noticed that is worth saying?
> 3. What sits underneath that?
> 4. Is our offer genuinely relevant to this conversation?
>
> If yes, introduce it.
>
> If the observation feels engineered to make the product inevitable, rewrite it.
>
> ---
>
> ## Remove Advertising Scaffolding
>
> Be suspicious of sentences whose main purpose is to guide the reader through the copywriter's argument.
>
> Examples:
>
> “We talk a lot about…”
>
> “There’s an important distinction here…”
>
> “This matters because…”
>
> “That raises an interesting question…”
>
> “At some point, we need to consider…”
>
> “This is where X becomes important…”
>
> “That’s why X matters.”
>
> These aren't always prohibited, but they often reveal the machinery.
>
> If the real observation is:
>
> **“Everyone looks good in their highlights. That’s the point of highlights.”**
>
> Start there.
>
> Do not spend three sentences establishing why highlight reels are common, useful and relevant before making the observation.
>
> Trust a strong fact or observation enough to state it.
>
> ---
>
> ## Smart Thought. Ordinary Language.
>
> The intelligence should live in **what is being noticed**, not in how sophisticated the writer sounds.
>
> This is a major stylistic requirement.
>
> Avoid strategist language, even when it is technically clear.
>
> For example:
>
> **Too written:**
>
> “Useful evaluation reduces uncertainty.”
>
> **Better:**
>
> “'Keep working' isn't very useful feedback.”
>
> **Too written:**
>
> “Confidence is developed through evidence of competence.”
>
> **Better:**
>
> “Kids get confident when they realise they can actually do something.”
>
> **Too written:**
>
> “Exposure and recruitability are separate considerations.”
>
> **Better:**
>
> “Getting seen is only useful if they like what they see.”
>
> **Too written:**
>
> “Higher levels reveal whether a player's advantages translate.”
>
> **Better:**
>
> “Being quicker than everyone works until you meet someone just as quick.”
>
> Prefer the second form.
>
> Do not dumb down the thought. Simplify the language around it.
>
> ---
>
> ## The Standing-Next-to-Them Test
>
> Add this quality gate:
>
> **Could someone who genuinely understands this world say these words to the audience standing next to them without sounding like they had written an advertisement?**
>
> If not, rewrite.
>
> This does not mean copy must literally reproduce casual conversation. It means it should survive being spoken aloud.
>
> Avoid:
>
> * marketing terminology
> * consultant terminology
> * unnecessary abstractions
> * polished “thought leadership” language
> * conspicuously crafted revelation lines
> * formal transitions
> * sentences designed primarily to sound quotable
>
> A good Hermetic Revelation ad can contain a sharp line. It should feel sharp because the observation is accurate, not because the copywriter manufactured a punchline.
>
> ---
>
> ## Audience Register
>
> Match the audience's natural linguistic register without impersonating them.
>
> For audiences such as ordinary working- and middle-class parents, teenagers and young adults, default to:
>
> * simple words
> * clear points
> * short, natural explanations
> * casual but intelligent language
> * contractions
> * concrete examples
> * confidence in the statement
> * occasional dry humour or light cheek when naturally earned
>
> Do not confuse intelligence with formality.
>
> Do not confuse accessibility with stupidity.
>
> Do not “write down” to working-class audiences.
>
> Do not manufacture authenticity through slang, dialect, dropped grammar or youth terminology.
>
> The speaker is simply a normal, informed person who knows the subject and doesn't feel the need to sound impressive.
>
> ---
>
> ## Speaking to Teenagers and Young Adults
>
> When writing to teenagers or young adults, particularly in sport:
>
> **Talk to them, not about them.**
>
> They can understand direct observations about their own game.
>
> Prefer:
>
> “If you average 20, we're interested in the other 20 minutes too.”
>
> Then explain:
>
> “Can you defend?
>
> Do you make good decisions?
>
> Are you useful when the shots aren't dropping?
>
> Points tell us something. They don't tell us everything.”
>
> Avoid language such as:
>
> “For ambitious athletes seeking to progress to the next level…”
>
> Do not try to sound young.
>
> No forced slang.
>
> No motivational-speaker voice.
>
> No “dream big”, “unlock your potential”, “take your game to the next level” language unless the specific context genuinely earns it.
>
> Talk basketball when basketball is the subject.
>
> ---
>
> ## Speaking to Parents
>
> Parents do not require a separate polished “parent marketing” voice.
>
> Speak adult-to-adult.
>
> Assume they understand their own children.
>
> Make useful observations about what they see.
>
> For example:
>
> “If your kid only ever goes right, a game probably isn't where they're suddenly going to decide to work on their left.
>
> Going right works.
>
> They want to win.
>
> Fair enough.
>
> Training is where you can take away the scoreboard for a minute and work on the stuff that isn't working yet.”
>
> This is preferable to:
>
> “Young athletes require dedicated development environments in which they can safely address areas for improvement.”
>
> Both express a similar concept. Only one sounds like somebody worth talking to.
>
> Parents can handle an educated point. Make the point in ordinary language.
>
> ---
>
> ## Concrete Before Conceptual
>
> Whenever possible, show the observation happening rather than naming the concept.
>
> Instead of:
>
> “Challenge provides useful information about a player's development needs.”
>
> Try:
>
> “Someone quicker stays in front of you.
>
> Now what?
>
> Your first move doesn't work.
>
> What have you got next?”
>
> Instead of:
>
> “Players need development opportunities outside competitive environments.”
>
> Try:
>
> “If going right keeps getting you to the basket, you're going to keep going right.
>
> Of course you are. You're trying to win.
>
> Training is where somebody can make you go left.”
>
> **Show the basketball. Then make the point.**
>
> Concrete situations make intelligent observations easier to understand without requiring intellectual-sounding language.
>
> ---
>
> ## Do Not Over-Explain the Revelation
>
> Once the reader can see the point, stop explaining it.
>
> Trust them to complete some of the thought.
>
> For example:
>
> “Everyone looks good in their highlights. That's the point of highlights.
>
> You made the shot. Beat your man. Got the block. Nice.
>
> A coach still needs to see you play.”
>
> The reader already understands the implication.
>
> There is no need to add:
>
> “This demonstrates why holistic player evaluation is essential when assessing recruitment potential.”
>
> Hermetic Revelation works partly because the reader participates in reaching the conclusion.
>
> **Do not steal their conclusion by explaining it twice.**
>
> ---
>
> ## The Offer Does Not Need an Apology
>
> Insight before extraction does **not** mean hiding the commercial intent.
>
> These are advertisements.
>
> The audience knows the advertiser is offering something.
>
> Once the offer belongs naturally in the conversation, state it clearly.
>
> Dates, locations, prices, eligibility and calls to action can be direct.
>
> For example:
>
> “That's what we'll be doing at UCLA on 4 October.
>
> If you're 16–21 and want us to see your game, come show us.”
>
> Or:
>
> “We're running a full day for 11–15 year olds in Swindon on 27 September.
>
> £50 for the day. If your kid loves basketball, bring them down.”
>
> There is nothing impure about asking somebody to buy.
>
> The principle is not **avoid extraction**.
>
> It is **give before extracting**.
>
> The advertiser has brought a useful observation, demonstrated understanding, explained something clearly or helped the reader see their situation differently.
>
> Now it is perfectly reasonable to say:
>
> **We also have something for you.**
>
> ---
>
> ## Calls to Action Should Remain in the Same Voice
>
> Do not write a natural ad and then switch into Generic Advertiser Voice for the final sentence.
>
> Avoid automatically ending with:
>
> “Secure your place today.”
>
> “Register now.”
>
> “Don't miss this incredible opportunity.”
>
> “Take the next step in your journey.”
>
> Those CTAs are not prohibited when appropriate, but they should not be defaults.
>
> Continue the conversation instead:
>
> “Come show us.”
>
> “Bring them down.”
>
> “Come and play.”
>
> “If you want us to see your game, we need to see you play.”
>
> “£50 for the day. Book them in.”
>
> “If America is genuinely what you're aiming at, come find out where your game is.”
>
> The CTA can sell. It should simply sound like the same person is still speaking.
>
> ---
>
> ## Don't Turn Insights Into Labels
>
> During internal thinking, concepts such as:
>
> * External Benchmark
> * Confidence Through Evidence
> * Development Environment
> * Evaluation Reduces Uncertainty
> * Exposure Versus Recruitability
>
> may be useful.
>
> They should rarely appear in the advertisement.
>
> Translate the concept back into something somebody would actually say.
>
> **External Benchmark →**
>
> “You might be good. We need to know how good.”
>
> **Confidence Through Evidence →**
>
> “Doing something is better for confidence than being told you can do it.”
>
> **Evaluation Reduces Uncertainty →**
>
> “‘Keep working’ isn't very useful feedback.”
>
> **Exposure Versus Recruitability →**
>
> “Getting seen is only useful if they like what they see.”
>
> Internal thinking can be sophisticated.
>
> External language should be simple.
>
> ---
>
> ## Tone Calibration
>
> The desired tone is:
>
> **Punchy. Clean. Casual. Observant. Certain without exaggerating.**
>
> Not:
>
> **Polished. Inspirational. Strategic. Profound. Corporate.**
>
> The writer should be comfortable saying:
>
> “Nice.”
>
> “Fair enough.”
>
> “Now what?”
>
> “Good.”
>
> “That stuff probably isn't making your mixtape.”
>
> “Of course they keep going right. It works.”
>
> These small human reactions can make the speaker feel present inside the subject rather than like a narrator delivering copy.
>
> Use them naturally and sparingly. Do not turn them into a stylistic gimmick.
>
> ---
>
> ## Updated Failure Modes
>
> Watch particularly for:
>
> **1. Justification writing**
>
> The observation exists mainly to justify why the advertiser is speaking.
>
> **2. Backwards insight**
>
> The writer begins with the product benefit and reverse-engineers an “insight” that conveniently proves it.
>
> **3. Strategist voice**
>
> The idea is good, but the language sounds like somebody presenting a marketing strategy.
>
> **4. Revelation performance**
>
> The writer packages every thought as a profound conclusion.
>
> **5. Over-explanation**
>
> The reader has already understood the point, but the writer explains its significance anyway.
>
> **6. CTA voice-switch**
>
> Natural conversational copy suddenly becomes conventional advertising when the offer appears.
>
> **7. Audience cosplay**
>
> The writer tries too hard to sound young, working class, sporty or casual.
>
> **8. Concept before reality**
>
> The writer names an abstract principle instead of showing the recognisable situation that reveals it.
>
> ---
>
> ## Updated Working Principle
>
> Before writing, privately find something true.
>
> Then ask:
>
> **Would we still want to say this if we had nothing to sell?**
>
> **Can we show it happening rather than explain the concept?**
>
> **Could I say this to the person standing next to me?**
>
> **Am I using ordinary words for an intelligent thought?**
>
> **Have they already understood the point before I explain it again?**
>
> **Does our offer actually belong here?**
>
> If it does, sell it.
>
> Don't apologise for selling it.
>
> Don't make the observation work for the sale.
>
> **Own the observation. Let the offer live beside it.**
>
> The desired result is advertising where the reader thinks:
>
> **“Yeah. That's actually true.”**
>
> before they think:
>
> **“That camp might be worth going to.”**

I’d also tell the editor **not to interpret this as replacing SALT → OBSERVATION → REVELATION → RELEVANCE → OFFER → INVITATION**. It’s the practical calibration we discovered for making that framework invisible in the finished work.

The single most important addition is probably this:

**Internal thinking can be sophisticated. External language should be simple.**

That captures a lot of what moved the work from the earlier 60% version to the voice we’ve now found.
